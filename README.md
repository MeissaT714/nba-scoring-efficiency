# sports-data-analysis-projects
Python analysis of scoring volume vs efficiency in the NBA 2025-26 season and historical MVP seasons
## Tools and Data
Built with Python using pandas and matplotlib. Player and team data sourced from Basketball Reference. Charts include team logos sourced from ESPN CDN
Scoring Volume vs. Efficiency in the NBA: Does Taking More Shots Come at a Cost?
## Introduction
In basketball, the best scorers are often the ones who take the most shots. But does commanding a higher share of your team's offensive possessions come at the cost of efficiency? This project uses Python and data from Basketball Reference to analyze the relationship between usage rate (USG%) and true shooting percentage (TS%) for NBA players in the 2025-26 season, and extends that analysis to every MVP season since Iverson in 2001 to understand how the profile of elite scorers has changed over time.
Usage rate measures the percentage of team possessions a player uses while on the floor. True shooting percentage accounts for two-pointers, three-pointers, and free throws to give a more complete picture of scoring efficiency than field goal percentage alone.
### 2025-26 Season Findings
Analyzing 49 NBA players who logged at least 1800 minutes and a usage rate of 22% or higher in the 2025-26 season, a slight negative relationship between volume and efficiency emerges, but it is weaker than conventional wisdom might suggest. High usage players do tend to have marginally lower true shooting percentages, but the variance around that trend is enormous. Some of the league's highest usage players are also among its most efficient scorers.
Shai Gilgeous-Alexander and Nikola Jokić stand out as the clearest examples of elite scorers who maintain exceptional efficiency despite commanding a high share of their team's possessions. Jaylen Brown, on the other hand, represents the opposite extreme — the league's second highest usage player at over 36%, but his efficiency sits slightly below the median rather than at the top. This summer, an interesting discussion centered around Brown’s analytical value. The star was traded to the 76ers for Paul George’s massive contract and just two first round picks, which surprised many fans given that he finished 6th in MVP voting and it was thought he would be worth much more. Analytically, however, his combination of high usage rate, low efficiency, and weak assist-to-turnover ratio sparked much discourse, with an anonymous NBA data analyst saying they viewed him as “the seventh-best player on a team.” While this might be overkill, it provides an interesting lens into the hyper-optimization of today’s NBA, and these opinions have some basis, since, for example, the Celtics have played at a 70-win pace (36-6) in the 42 games Brown has missed over the past three seasons. Overall, this chart suggests that while volume and efficiency are related, a player's skill set and shot selection matter far more than usage rate alone.
One important limitation of this analysis is that true shooting percentage does not fully account for shot difficulty or creation burden. A center finishing lobs at the rim and a guard creating off the dribble against a set defense may post similar TS% numbers, but they are doing very different things. This positional bias should be kept in mind when interpreting the chart.
#### Historical MVP Findings
Plotting every NBA MVP season since Allen Iverson's 2001 award reveals a striking trend: the league's best players have become dramatically more efficient over time, even as their usage rates have remained relatively stable.
Iverson's 2001 MVP season — one of the most celebrated individual performances in NBA history — featured a TS% of just .518, which would place him in the bottom quarter of today's high-usage players. Tim Duncan's back-to-back MVP seasons in 2002 and 2003 show similarly modest efficiency by modern standards. By contrast, recent MVPs like Jokić, Stephen Curry in 2016, and Gilgeous-Alexander in 2025 post TS% figures above .660, numbers that would have been nearly unimaginable for a high-volume scorer two decades ago.
This shift reflects broader changes in how the NBA is played. The three-point revolution, the decline of mid-range inefficiency, and more sophisticated offensive systems have raised the efficiency floor for elite players leaguewide. Russell Westbrook's 2017 MVP season is the notable outlier. He had the highest usage rate ever at above 41%, and paired with below-average efficiency, his season sparked significant debate about what the MVP award should reward. Many argued James Harden was more deserving, as his Rockets won 7 more games while posting a 6% higher TS%, but Westbrook's historic triple-double average and weaker supporting cast ultimately won over voters.
Nikola Jokić's three MVP seasons are the clearest argument that volume and efficiency are not fundamentally at odds. Across 2021, 2022, and 2024, Jokić maintained elite TS% figures at high usage while also ranking near the very top of the league in assists and rebounds, which is a combination no other MVP in this dataset comes close to replicating.
##### Conclusion
The data suggests that scoring volume does carry a modest efficiency cost on average, but the relationship is far from deterministic. What separates elite scorers from inefficient ball hogs is not how many shots they take, but how well they select and create those shots. The evolution of MVP seasons since 2001 shows that the modern NBA rewards players who can do both — carry a heavy offensive load while maintaining elite efficiency. Jokić, Curry, and Gilgeous-Alexander represent the new archetype: high usage, high efficiency, and high basketball IQ working together rather than in opposition.
